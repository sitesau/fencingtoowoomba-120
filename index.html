Match the material to the job, not the mood board

The source guide treats material choice as a use case decision. Colorbond is described as the workhorse for boundary fencing because it avoids painting and its colour range sits quietly near brick and render. Timber paling still suits character streets, while lapped and capped timber is the step up when gaps are the issue. PVC removes painting altogether, aluminium slat fencing reads more like a modern frontage than full privacy screening, and glass pool fencing is treated as compliance work before style.

That means privacy fencing, chain wire, tubular steel, gates and automation should be compared by function: sightlines, premises security, yard or school boundaries, and the gate that must hang off the same line.

Boundary duty can change the quote conversation

Where the fence is on the boundary, the source frames it as a dividing fence and says Queensland has a cost sharing process. Adjoining owners generally contribute equally to a sufficient dividing fence, while the owner wanting something taller or more premium pays the difference. The practical order is talk first, take a written quote to the neighbour, then use the formal notice path if agreement stalls.

The guide says the notice to contribute names the land, fence line, work and estimated cost, with at least one written quotation attached. If there is no agreement within one month, either owner can apply to QCAT within two months of the notice.

Height, retaining walls and pools are separate triggers

Keep two approval signals apart. A fence up to 2 m above natural ground is generally accepted development in Queensland; above that height it becomes building work and needs certifier approval. Where a fence sits on a retaining wall, the source says the combined height is measured, so the wall and fence are assessed together.

Pool fencing follows a different path. It is built to the Queensland pool safety standard, with glass and aluminium pool barriers described as compliant options. Sharper quote questions are which part forms the barrier, how gates are handled, what existing structures affect the line, and whether the price covers repair, replacement or a new barrier.

Put like quotes beside like quotes

A fair comparison does not need hidden assumptions. Put the same fence line, material class, removal requirements, access limits and gate or pool barrier inclusions beside each other. If one quote answers repair and another answers replacement, they are solving different problems. The source also names common sticking points: nobody arriving to measure, and the neighbour discussion not yet happening. A written quote makes the talk concrete because each owner can see material, run length, removal, access and estimated cost.

  1. Measure the run. Record location, length, old fence removal, failed posts or rails, and access limits.
  2. Name special features. Add corner blocks, retaining walls, pool areas and driveway gates before pricing starts.
  3. Take written quotes. Compare the same line, material class, removal, access and gate or pool inclusions.
  4. Talk to the neighbour. For a dividing fence, discuss cost sharing and use the notice path if agreement stalls.
  5. Check triggers. Confirm QBCC threshold, 2 m height treatment, retaining wall measurement and pool safety scope.

Common questions

When is a QBCC licensed contractor needed? The source guide says paid Queensland fencing work valued at $3,300 or more needs a QBCC licensed contractor, while some owner built fences are exempt.

Does every fence over 2 m need approval? A fence above 2 m above natural ground is described as building work needing certifier approval, while up to that height it is generally accepted development.

What if the neighbour wants a fancier boundary fence? The source says adjoining owners generally contribute equally to a sufficient dividing fence, and the owner wanting more than sufficient pays the difference.
